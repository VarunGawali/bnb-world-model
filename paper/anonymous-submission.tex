\begin{abstract}
Modern Mixed-Integer Programming solvers rely on Branch-and-Cut, which must
make sequential decisions: which variable to branch on, which cuts to add,
which node to explore, at every step of an exponentially large search tree.
Because each decision reshapes the subsequent search, methods that score
candidates from the current node alone are blind to the very signal that makes
their target expert, strong branching, effective. We present CutWorld, a latent dynamics model that learns to simulate the downstream consequences of
Branch-and-Cut decisions in latent space, replacing expensive LP solves with a
single cheap forward pass. Our model encodes each search node as a bipartite
graph using a GATv2 network with learned cross-attention pooling, then rolls
forward a causal Transformer to predict how both the global state and each
variable's representation evolve under a candidate action, enabling the
branching policy to be re-applied to imagined states for multi-step planning.
Candidates are scored by a MuZero-style return combining predicted per-step
rewards, a value estimate, and a cost-to-go penalty; the same latent drives
node ordering and informs both a learned and a parameter-free Gomory cut
selection rule within a single model that provably preserves solver exactness. Across medium ($500{\times}1000$, 300\,s) and hard ($1000{\times}2000$,
600\,s) Set Cover tiers, every neural variant exhausts its search tree on all
instances while every classical branching rule times out; a 3-step latent
rollout reduces explored nodes by 44\% over the imitation policy at medium
tier and by 14\% at hard tier (SGM 3{,}197 vs.\ 5{,}733 and 4{,}007
vs.\ 4{,}633). Neural-Best is up to 41\% faster than most-fractional on hard
instances, the tier where the planning advantage compounds most. We compare
against most-fractional and classical strong-branching heuristics (SB-1/4/8)
with component-wise ablations isolating each planning element.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A modern optimization solver makes millions of discrete sequential choices to solve a single instance, like which variable
to branch on, which cutting planes to add, which open node to explore next,
each one steering an enormous combinatorial search, and each governed today by a
hand-engineered heuristic. Model-based planning agents such as MuZero and
Dreamer \citep{schrittwieser2020mastering,hafner2020dream} have shown that
learning a model of environment dynamics, and planning by imagining the
consequences of actions, transforms sequential decision making, from Atari and
Go to continuous control. We bring this idea inside an optimization solver:
\emph{CutWorld}, a latent dynamics model for unified Branch-and-Cut control.

Mixed-Integer Programming (MIP) is a cornerstone of combinatorial optimization,
and modern solvers attack it with Branch-and-Cut. Starting from a linear
programming (LP) relaxation, B\&C repeatedly executes two core operations at
each search tree node: adding cutting planes, valid inequalities that tighten
the relaxation without removing integer solutions, and branching on a fractional
variable to partition the search space.

Unfortunately, making high-quality decisions at every node is computationally
expensive. The gold standard for branching, \emph{strong branching}, tentatively
solves the child LPs of each candidate variable to measure its bound
improvement; cut selection faces an analogous trade-off when choosing among
large pools of valid inequalities. To amortize these costs, recent machine
learning approaches learn to imitate strong branching in a single forward pass,
e.g., with graph neural networks \citep{gasse2019exact,khalil2016learning}, or
use reinforcement learning to select cuts \citep{tang2020reinforcement}. These
methods are effective, but they share a structural limitation: each scores a
candidate \emph{from the current node alone}, discarding the lookahead
signal.

CutWorld closes this gap with a learned latent dynamics model. We encode each
search node as a bipartite graph with a GATv2 network and attention readout,
and train a causal Transformer to predict how the latent and per-variable
embeddings evolve under a candidate action, so the branching policy can be
re-applied to imagined states for a k-step rollout. Candidates are scored by a
MuZero-style return combining predicted rewards, a value estimate, and a
cost-to-go penalty; Gomory cuts are selected via cross-attention over the same
encoder representations. A single unified model drives variable branching, cut
selection, and node ordering while preserving the solver's exactness guarantee.

\paragraph{Summary of Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A latent dynamics model for Branch-and-Cut.} We learn a
  solver's decision dynamics, so that branching and cutting are \emph{planned
  in imagination} rather than chosen by fixed heuristics, replacing the child
  LP solves that strong branching requires with a short latent rollout
  per candidate.

  \item \textbf{Unified control with exactness guarantees.} We show that a
  single latent model can simultaneously drive variable branching, node
  selection, and cut selection while strictly maintaining the solver's
  exact optimality guarantees.

  \item \textbf{Empirical validation.} On Set Cover across medium and hard
  tiers we report termination rate, explored nodes, wall-clock solving time,
  and cuts applied, with an ablation isolating each planning component's
  contribution against most-fractional and classical strong-branching
  heuristics.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

Learning to make solver decisions is an active area \citep{bengio2021machine}.
The dominant approach is imitation: \citet{khalil2016learning} regress
strong-branching scores from hand-crafted features, and \citet{gasse2019exact}
represent a node as a bipartite constraint-variable graph and train a graph
network to imitate strong branching in a single forward pass. This amortizes the
cost of strong branching but discards the very signal that makes it strong, the quality of the subtree each candidate ultimately induces. The bipartite
encoder of \citet{gasse2019exact} is the node representation we build on; we
replace its global mean pooling with a learned attention readout
\citep{lee2019set} over variable nodes, producing a graph-level state that
emphasizes fractional and high-objective variables rather than averaging
uniformly. Reinforcement learning restores a longer horizon:
\citet{scavuzzo2022learning} train a branching policy with tree-structured
returns, and \citet{nair2020solving} scale neural MIP solving with learned
diving and branching. These methods optimize long-term reward, but remain
\emph{model-free}: they treat the solver as a black box and never learn how a
decision reshapes the subsequent search state. The theoretical conditions under
which learned branching rules preserve solver correctness are studied by
\citet{chengbasu2023theoretical}, motivating our design that gates all learned
decisions behind bound-justified pruning rather than letting them alter
feasibility or optimality tests. Graph neural networks have also been applied
directly to Set Cover \citep{shafi2022graphscp}, the benchmark domain we
evaluate on.

Cut selection has been learned along the same lines. \citet{tang2020reinforcement}
cast it as an RL problem, and \citet{paulus2022learning} imitate a look-ahead
expert, but the look-ahead only produces training labels; at inference the
policy predicts from the current node alone. \citet{wang2023hierarchical} use
a hierarchical sequence model; like prior cut methods, selection is myopic.
Learned large neighbourhood search \citep{sonnerat2021learning} targets yet
another solver decision. Across branching and cutting, prior work scores
candidates myopically or through an expensive look-ahead the learned policy
cannot itself invoke.

A complementary line of work introduces sequence models over the
\emph{realized} branching history. \citet{zhang2026mambabranchin} encode the
root-to-node sequence with a Mamba SSM, training contrastively on productive
vs.\ unproductive histories. This \emph{retrospective} approach captures how
the search arrived, but cannot see what a candidate will cause downstream.
CutWorld takes the opposite, \emph{prospective} stance: it simulates
consequences before committing, and additionally handles cut selection and node
ordering from the same latent.

CutWorld draws on model-based reinforcement learning. MuZero
\citep{schrittwieser2020mastering} plans without reconstructing observations,
and Dreamer \citep{hafner2020dream} learns behaviors in latent space; our
DynamicsTransformer follows the same design, with latent overshooting as a
training-time stabilizer. We combine the bipartite representation of
\citet{gasse2019exact} with a learned latent forward model: rather than paying for
explicit LP look-ahead, we imagine the downstream consequences of a decision at
the cost of a short latent rollout per candidate, and drive branching, cut
selection, and node ordering from a single latent.

\section{Background and Problem Formulation}

A (binary) Mixed-Integer Program minimizes $c^\top x$ subject to $Ax\ge b$,
$x\in\{0,1\}^n$. Set Cover is the case $A\in\{0,1\}^{m\times n}$,
$b=\mathbf{1}$. Branch-and-Cut solves an LP relaxation at each node; if
fractional it either adds a valid cut $\alpha^\top x\ge\beta$ or branches on a
fractional variable $x_j$, creating subproblems $x_j{=}0$ and $x_j{=}1$.

We model the search as an MDP: a state $s_t$ is a search node; an action $a_t$
branches on a variable or adds a cut; the reward $r_t$ is the dual-bound
improvement. Evaluating the true transition requires solving child LPs, so
prior methods avoid look-ahead. CutWorld learns a latent surrogate: an encoder
maps $s_t$ to a latent, and a dynamics model predicts the transition without
invoking the solver, turning look-ahead into a cheap forward pass.

\label{sec:exactness}
\begin{remark}[Exactness]
\label{prop:exact}
For any branching variable chosen among the fractional candidates, any subset of
globally valid cuts added at any node, and any ordering of the open nodes,
Branch-and-Cut returns a global optimum: branching partitions the integer
feasible set exhaustively, globally valid cuts preserve every integer-feasible
point, and node ordering affects only search sequence, not the
bound-and-integrality pruning rule. CutWorld selects only among these
correctness-preserving options, so it preserves the solver's exactness guarantee
for every setting of the learned policy.
\end{remark}

Our validity requirement drives the cut design in Section~\ref{sec:method}: we
generate Gomory fractional cuts, which are valid by construction, and let the
model \emph{select} among them rather than invent inequalities.

We view Branch-and-Cut as a sequential decision process. At each node $t$ of
the search, the solver observes a state $s_t$ (the current relaxation), takes an
action $a_t$ (branch on a fractional variable, or add a cut), and transitions to
the next node. We learn (i)~an encoder that maps $s_t$ to a latent state,
(ii)~a dynamics model that predicts how that latent evolves under an action, and
(iii)~heads that score decisions by rolling the dynamics forward
(Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). Throughout, $d=128$ denotes the embedding dimension.

\subsection{Encoder}

Each node is a bipartite graph over the constraints and variables of the
relaxation: variable nodes carry 19 LP-derived features (solution value,
fractionality, reduced cost, bound and basis status), constraint nodes carry 5
features (zero-padded to 19 in a shared feature matrix), and each edge carries
its coefficient together with two derived features, the RHS-normalized value and
the sign. A 3-layer bidirectional GATv2 network \citep{brody2022how} with 4
attention heads (head dimension 32, concatenated to $d=128$) performs
two-directional message passing per layer, constraints-to-variables and
variables-to-constraints, with LayerNorm after each pass. GATv2 attention,
rather than mean aggregation, lets each variable weight the constraints that
actually bind it at the current node, which vary enormously across the tree.
A learned cross-attention readout (CrossAttentionPool) attends a single learned
query vector over the per-variable embeddings to produce the graph-level
summary, emphasising the variables most relevant at this node:
\begin{equation}
  \bigl(\{h_i\}_{i=1}^{n},\, z\bigr) = \mathrm{Enc}(s_t),
  \qquad h_i,\, z \in \mathbb{R}^{d};\quad a = h_v.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Latent Dynamics}

A 4-layer causal Transformer with 4 attention heads, hidden dimension $d=128$,
FFN width $4d$, maximum sequence length 512, and learned positional encodings
models the evolution of the latent along the root-to-node trajectory. Each step
produces one token encoding the current latent, the branched variable, and the
branch direction:
\begin{equation}
  u_\tau = W_{\mathrm{in}}[z_\tau \,\|\, a_\tau \,\|\, d_\tau],
  \quad \tau \le t,
\end{equation}
where $a_\tau = h_{v_\tau}$ is the embedding of the branched variable and
$d_\tau \in \{+1,-1\}$ is the branch direction. Conditioning on direction is
what lets a single model represent both children of a node. The next graph
latent is decoded with a residual connection:
\begin{equation}
  z_{t+1} = \mathrm{LN}\!\bigl(z_t + W_{\mathrm{out}}\,\mathrm{TF}(u_{0:t})\bigr).
\end{equation}
A per-variable module predicts how each variable embedding evolves.
The update is a two-layer MLP (layers $3d\!\to\!2d\!\to\!d$, GELU) applied to
the concatenated input $[h_i^{\,t}\,\|\,z_{t+1}\,\|\,a_t]$:
\begin{equation}
  h_i^{\,t+1} = \mathrm{LN}\!\bigl(
    h_i^{\,t} + \mathrm{MLP}_h([h_i^{\,t} \,\|\, z_{t+1} \,\|\, a_t])
  \bigr).
  \label{eq:varupdate}
\end{equation}
Predicting per-variable embeddings~\eqref{eq:varupdate} lets the branching
policy be re-applied to an imagined state during planning. We ground the latent with a linear head
$\hat{b}_{t+1}=w_b^\top z_{t+1}$ that predicts the node's normalized dual
bound, tying the imagined latent to a real solver quantity, and a reward head
$\hat{r}_{t}=w_r^\top z_{t+1}$ that predicts the per-step dual-bound
improvement.

\subsection{Value, Cost-to-Go, and Policy}

Let $\bar{h}_{\mathrm{frac}}$ be the mean embedding of the \emph{currently
fractional} variables only. The value and cost-to-go heads share this enriched
input, so each head sees what is actually undecided at this node rather than a
global average:
\begin{align}
  V(z) &= \mathrm{MLP}_V([z \,\|\, \bar{h}_{\mathrm{frac}}]), \\
  C(z) &= \mathrm{softplus}\bigl(\mathrm{MLP}_C([z \,\|\,
           \bar{h}_{\mathrm{frac}}])\bigr),
\end{align}
where $C$ predicts $\log(1+\text{remaining nodes})$ with a Monte-Carlo target
$T-t$ read directly from each trajectory. The branching policy is a
pointer network scoring each fractional variable jointly against the graph
context:
\begin{equation}
  \pi(v) \propto w^\top\!\tanh\!\bigl(W_k h_v + W_z z\bigr)
                 \odot W_q z \,/\, \sqrt{d}.
\end{equation}
An integrality head $\sigma\!\bigl(\mathrm{MLP}([z \,\|\, \mathrm{depth} \,\|\,
n_{\mathrm{frac}}])\bigr)$ predicts the probability that the next node is a
leaf, used to gate the rollout (skip if already near-integral) and to control
when the cut pipeline fires.

\subsection{Planning Rollout for Candidate Selection}

To branch, we take the policy's top-$k$ candidates and score each by imagining
the subtree it induces. Starting from the current $(z,\{h_i\})$ with first
action $a=h_c$, we unroll the dynamics for $K$ steps, re-applying the policy to
each predicted state to choose the next action
$v_j=\arg\max_{v\in\mathcal{F}}\pi(v\mid z_j,\{h^j\})$ over the fractional set
$\mathcal{F}$. The candidate is scored by a MuZero-style return that accumulates
predicted rewards along the imagined path, bootstraps with the value at the
leaf, and penalizes predicted remaining work:
\begin{equation}
  R(c) = \sum_{j=1}^{K}\gamma^{\,j-1}\hat{r}_j
       + \gamma^{\,K-1}V(z_K)
       - w_c\sum_{j=1}^{K}\gamma^{\,j-1}\hat{C}(z_j).
  \label{eq:return}
\end{equation}
We use a \emph{policy-anchored} rule: over the
candidate set we standardize the policy scores and the returns and select
$c^\star=\arg\max_c\bigl[\hat{\pi}(c)+\lambda\,\hat{R}(c)\bigr]$, so the
latent lookahead refines the policy's ranking instead of replacing it
($\lambda\!=\!0$ recovers the policy). If the top candidate's probability exceeds a threshold the
policy choice is taken directly, so the lookahead cost is paid only on
genuinely close decisions. The same value drives \emph{node selection}: open
nodes are ordered by ascending predicted cost-to-go $C(z)$, so the subtree
expected to close soonest is explored first.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:select} summarizes candidate selection.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Branching-variable selection by latent rollout}
\label{alg:select}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} node latent $z$, variable embeddings $\{h_i\}$,
       fractional set $\mathcal{F}$, depth $K$, factor $b$, weight $\lambda$
\State $\pi_c \gets \pi(\cdot\mid z,\{h_i\})$ over $\mathcal{F}$
\If{$\max_c \pi_c \ge \theta$} \Return $\arg\max_c \pi_c$ \EndIf
        \Comment{confident early exit}
\State $\mathcal{C}\gets \textsc{TopK}_k(\pi_c,\,\mathcal{F})$
\For{each candidate $c\in\mathcal{C}$}
  \State $R(c)\gets \textsc{Rollout}(z,\{h_i\},c,K,b)$
         \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:return}}
\EndFor
\State $\hat\pi(c)\gets\mathrm{standardize}(\pi_c)$,\quad
       $\hat R(c)\gets\mathrm{standardize}(R(c))$
\State \Return $\arg\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\bigl[\hat\pi(c)+\lambda\,\hat R(c)\bigr]$
       \Comment{policy-anchored; $\lambda{=}0$ recovers policy}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Cutting-Plane Selection}

The model performs cut \emph{selection}; validity is guaranteed by the
generator. We generate a pool of Gomory fractional cuts at the root from the
LP optimal basis: each fractional basic variable yields one cut derived from
its tableau row. Because these cuts come from globally valid rows with no
branching bounds applied, they hold for every integer-feasible solution and
are safely inherited by all descendants, making the approach genuine
Branch-and-Cut rather than cut-and-branch.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Setup}

We evaluate on Set Cover instances across easy, medium, and hard tiers,
collecting strong-branching trajectories for training (full instance and
hyperparameter details in Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}). Baselines are
most-fractional and classical strong branching with reliability-branching
candidate counts of 1, 4, and 8 (SB-1/4/8), plus the imitation policy and
CutWorld ablation variants. Metrics: termination rate (neural methods exhaust
their search tree; classical time out), SGM nodes (shift $\delta_n{=}10$),
SGM wall time (shift $\delta_t{=}1$), and cutting planes. Termination is
reported first because node counts are only comparable across methods that
both finish their trees; classical SGM nodes are measured at the time limit
and marked $\dagger$.

\subsection{Training Details}
\label{sec:training}

Training uses AdamW with cosine LR schedule and mixed precision. The
curriculum has four stages, each warm-started from the previous checkpoint and
early-stopped on its own validation metric: (1)~\emph{policy imitation},
(2)~\emph{value}, (3)~\emph{dynamics} with a latent-overshooting horizon
ramped from 1 to 3 steps, and (4)~\emph{joint fine-tuning} under
\begin{equation}
  L = L_{\pi} + 0.5\,L_V + 0.1\,L_{\mathrm{int}} + 0.1\,L_{\mathrm{consist}}
      + 0.3\,L_S + 0.5\,L_C.
\end{equation}
Full hyperparameters are in Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}.

\subsection{Ablation}

To understand where any improvement comes from, we ablate the planner one
component at a time, starting from the imitation policy alone and successively
enabling the MF-blend heuristic, Gomory cut selection, 3-step latent rollout,
rollout with a size penalty, and the full model (rollout + cuts).

\subsection{Cut Selection}

The contribution of root Gomory cut selection is visible directly in
Tables~\ref{tab:main} and~\ref{tab:hard}. Adding attention-selected cuts to
the imitation policy (Policy$+$Cuts) yields a modest $-6\%$ node reduction at
medium tier ($5{,}396$ vs.\ $5{,}733$), consistent with the root LP already
being relatively tight on these instances. The more consequential effect is on
\emph{solution quality}: combining cuts with rollout (Neural-Best)
reduces the primal gap from $5.2\%$ (Rollout alone) to $1.9\%$ at medium
tier and from $3.2\%$ to $1.4\%$ at hard tier — rollout shrinks the tree,
cuts tighten the LP bound, and together they close most of the gap to
classical methods.

\subsection{Discussion}


\textbf{Termination is the primary gain.}
Every neural variant exhausts its search tree on $100\%$ of instances at both tiers; every classical
rule exhausts the time budget without finishing the tree. The neural wall-time advantage is up to $41\%$ at hard tier and $25\%$ at
medium, sufficient in both cases to determine whether a solve terminates. Classical node counts (marked $\dagger$) are measured at timeout
and are lower bounds on a completed solve, so they are not directly comparable
to neural termination counts.

\textbf{Rollout reduces nodes; rollout + cuts recover solution quality.}
3-step rollout cuts SGM nodes by $44\%$ vs.\ imitation alone ($3{,}197$
vs.\ $5{,}733$ at medium). Imitation-only branching (Policy) finds worse
integer solutions than classical methods despite fewer nodes: gap $+4.3\%$
(medium) and $+2.2\%$ (hard) vs.\ MF's $+1.2\%$ and $+1.0\%$. Neural-Best
(rollout + cuts) closes this to $+1.9\%$ / $+1.4\%$ — the best neural
configuration and within $<1$\,pp of classical methods that do not terminate.
Rollout thus serves two roles: fewer branching decisions \emph{and} better
integer solutions.

\textbf{Size head has scale-dependent limits.}
The subtree-size head reverses its easy-tier gain at medium
($+35\%$) and hard ($+9\%$): it extrapolates beyond its training distribution
and marks a concrete limit of the current curriculum.

\textbf{Termination criterion and optimality.}
Neural methods terminate by exhausting the search tree, not by proving a
dual-bound gap of zero; LP-infeasible nodes are silently pruned, so the heap
can empty before the tree is truly complete. The 100\% termination rate
reflects tree-traversal efficiency, not proven optimality. The gap column
($\ddagger$) is relative to the best feasible solution found by any method
within the same time budget; no verified optima exist within the evaluated
limits.

\section{Conclusion}

We have presented CutWorld, a latent dynamics model for Branch-and-Cut that
plans branching, node selection, and root Gomory cut selection jointly in latent
space, replacing the child LP solves that strong branching requires with a short
latent rollout per candidate, all while preserving the solver's
exactness guarantee. The empirical record across two difficulty tiers supports
four conclusions. First, the tree-exhaustion discontinuity is real and consistent:
every neural variant exhausts its search tree on $100\%$ of instances at both tiers within budget;
every classical rule exhausts the budget without finishing the tree. The neural wall-time advantage reaches $41\%$ over most-fractional at hard
tier and $25\%$ at medium, consistently falling on the correct side of the
budget cutoff. Second, 3-step latent rollout is
the dominant planning component, reducing explored nodes by $44\%$ over the
imitation policy at medium tier and by $14\%$ at hard tier. Third, rollout does more than reduce nodes: it recovers solution
quality lost by imitation. The imitation policy alone reaches $+4.3\%$ primal
gap at medium tier and $+2.2\%$ at hard tier; the full model (rollout + cuts)
closes this to $+1.9\%$ and $+1.4\%$ respectively, within $0.8$\,pp and
$0.7$\,pp of classical methods that do not terminate. Fourth, the subtree-size
head does not generalise beyond the training distribution, reversing its
easy-tier gain at both medium and hard tier; expanding the training distribution
is the most direct path to closing this gap. We view CutWorld as the first
realized instance of a broader agenda: learned dynamics models for optimization,
in which a solver's sequential decisions are planned in imagination rather than
resolved by fixed heuristics. Branch-and-Cut is the hardest setting for this
idea, exact tree search under a strict correctness constraint, yet the
core pattern holds. The same machinery applies wherever a solver makes a
sequence of discrete choices, and extending it to improvement search, where a
forward model imagines the effect of re-optimizing a chosen neighborhood, is a
natural next step that carries none of the exactness constraints of tree search.
